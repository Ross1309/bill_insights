How a bill becomes law

The steps every bill takes, what each one means, and where you'll see it on this site. Most bills stop at step 2: in the 118th Congress (2023–24), 16,565 bills were introduced, 962 passed the House or the Senate, 290 passed both and 274 became law.

  1. 1

    Introduced

    Any member can introduce a bill in their own chamber. It gets the next number: H.R. for House bills, S. for Senate bills. A bill has one sponsor and often many cosponsors who sign on to show support.

    On this site: every bill page shows its sponsor and cosponsor count; the stage tracker starts here.

  2. 2

    Committee

    The bill is sent to the (or committees) for its subject. Committees hold hearings, rewrite bills in a , and decide which ones to send to the full chamber ( them). This is where most bills stop: not voted down, just never taken up.

    On this site: most bills show “In committee”. The Bills tab starts with “Moving or passed” for that reason.

  3. 3

    A vote in the first chamber

    In the House, most important bills come to the floor under a : a resolution, adopted by majority vote first, that sets how long debate lasts and which amendments are allowed. Less controversial bills use a fast track, , which needs two-thirds to pass.

    In the Senate, any senator can keep debate going. Ending it takes : 60 votes for most legislation. Many bills instead pass by , with no recorded vote at all.

    Along the way there may be votes on , a or a . Only the final vote decides whether the bill passes.

    On this site: the Votes tab’s “Key votes” are those final votes; “All roll calls” adds the steps on the way. Bills passed by voice vote or unanimous consent have no roll call; their page says so.

  4. 4

    The other chamber

    The bill goes to the other chamber, which starts again: committee, then the floor. It can pass the bill as it is, change it, or never take it up.

    On this site: the tracker shows “Passed in the House; next: the Senate” (or the reverse).

  5. 5

    Agreeing on the same text

    Both chambers must pass exactly the same text. If the second chamber changed it, the first can the changes, the chambers can trade amendments, or a conference committee of members from both writes a compromise that each chamber then votes on.

    On this site: votes to concur show as “agreed to the Senate’s changes to …”.

  6. 6

    The President

    The final, bill is , who has 10 days (Sundays excepted) to sign it or it. If the President does neither, it becomes law without a signature, unless Congress has adjourned in the meantime: then it doesn’t (a “pocket veto”).

    Congress can override a veto with a two-thirds vote in both chambers.

    On this site: the tracker’s “To the President” step; a veto shows in red, and override votes read “voted to override the veto of …”.

  7. 7

    Law

    A signed (or overridden) bill becomes a , numbered by Congress and order: Public Law 119-60 is the 60th law of the 119th Congress. A Congress lasts two years; bills that haven’t passed by then die and must be introduced again. Many laws are signed in a term’s last weeks.

    On this site: “Became law” on bill pages, Home’s “Recently became law”, and the laws chart on This Congress.

Not every measure is a bill

  • Joint resolutions (H.J.Res., S.J.Res.) work like bills and can become law. One exception: a joint resolution proposing a constitutional amendment needs two-thirds of both chambers, skips the President and goes to the states; three-fourths of them (38) must ratify it.
  • Concurrent resolutions (H.Con.Res., S.Con.Res.) pass both chambers but don’t go to the President and aren’t law: the budget resolution, for example.
  • Simple resolutions (H.Res., S.Res.) concern one chamber only: its own rules, honors, or the terms of debate for a bill.

How votes are taken

  • A records how each member voted (Yea, Nay, Present or Not Voting). Those are the votes this site shows member by member.
  • A or records only the result, so there’s no member-by-member record.
  • Most votes need a simple majority of those voting. The exceptions: two-thirds for suspension of the rules, veto overrides and constitutional amendments; 60 senators for most cloture votes.

How we count

Where every number on this site comes from, and the rules we use. If something looks wrong, report an error.

Sources and updates

  • Bills, actions, laws, CRS summaries and subjects, members: the Congress.gov API (Library of Congress).
  • House roll-call votes: the Office of the Clerk of the U.S. House, via Congress.gov. Senate roll-call votes: senate.gov's official vote records.
  • Campaign finance: the Federal Election Commission (OpenFEC). Congressional districts for an address: the U.S. Census Bureau geocoder.
  • Two automatic updates run every night (votes and members at 10:00 UTC, bills at 10:30 UTC). The footer shows when each last finished, and a notice appears if one hasn't succeeded in 36 hours. We only show what these sources publish; when a value isn't available, we say so instead of estimating.

Which bills are here

  • Not all of the ~20,000 bills introduced each Congress. We store every bill that became law or got a recorded floor vote, plus recently active bills: the 10 most recently updated on Congress.gov each night, and the ~2,000 most recently updated when the site launched.
  • The Bills tab lists them by the date of their latest action, newest first.
  • Names: we use the title Congress.gov displays. When that is a sentence ("To amend …", "An act to provide …") and the bill has an official short title, we show the short title (the latest one, e.g. as passed) with the full official title under it. Resolutions that would overturn an agency's rule (under the Congressional Review Act) all begin "Providing for congressional disapproval under chapter 8 of title 5 …", so we name them by what they overturn, in the title's own words: Overturning the [agency] rule "[rule]". Other disapproval resolutions (such as arms sales) read "Disapproving …".
  • Categories (Economy, Healthcare, Foreign Affairs, Immigration, Defense, the same as the Issues topics) come from the bill's policy area, which the Congressional Research Service (CRS) assigns.
  • Stage comes from the official action history: a chamber counts as passing a bill only when an action starts with "Passed/agreed to in House/Senate". "Became law" requires a law number or a "Became Public Law" action.
  • What happens next (bill cards and bill pages) states the general rules of the process for the bill's current stage. It isn't a prediction of what Congress will do.
  • Stage tracker (bill cards and bill pages): Introduced, Committee, each chamber, the President, Law. Simple resolutions (H.Res., S.Res.) need only their own chamber; concurrent resolutions both chambers; neither goes to the President. Constitutional amendments go to the states after both chambers, not the President (ratification isn't tracked). "Failed" and "Vetoed" are shown only when an official action says so. Bills we haven't stored get their stage from the same rule applied to their action history; with no stage data, no tracker is shown.
  • Moving or passed (the Bills tab's default): bills and resolutions that passed at least one chamber, sent to the President or became law. House rules, which only set the terms of debate for another bill, are left out. "All bills" shows everything we store.

Plain-language summaries

  • Written by an AI model (OpenAI gpt-4o-mini) only from the official CRS summary, and labeled with that source. The model is told to stay neutral and not add anything that isn't in the CRS text.
  • No CRS summary yet means no AI summary: the bill says CRS hasn't published one. We never summarize from a title alone. Waiting bills are re-checked every night in turn (25 a night, bills with new activity first), so a new CRS summary usually appears within days for a bill that's moving, and within about six weeks otherwise.

This Congress so far

  • Same point in the term: a Congress starts on January 3 of an odd year; "day N" is N days after that, so each Congress is compared with the last two at the same stage of their two-year terms.
  • Bills are measures that can become law (H.R., S., H.J.Res., S.J.Res.), counted by introduction date. Passed a chamber / both: the first "Passed/agreed to in House" or "in Senate" action. "Both" means each chamber passed a version; if the second changed the text, the two still have to agree before the bill goes to the President. Laws: the "Became Public/Private Law" action, including bills signed after a term ended.
  • Parties split: most Democrats voted the opposite way from most Republicans (Yea vs Nay); ties, quorum calls and Speaker elections are left out.
  • Sources: GovInfo's bill status files (the bulk record of every bill's actions) and Congress.gov for bills; the House Clerk and senate.gov for votes and party totals. The current Congress is updated nightly from Congress.gov.

Roll-call votes

  • Every recorded House and Senate vote of the current Congress. "Aye" and "No" (used on some House votes) are counted as Yea and Nay.
  • When a new Congress begins (January 3 of odd-numbered years): for the first weeks, totals, charts, topics and the weekly summary keep describing the previous Congress until the new one has 25 roll-call votes. The previous Congress's last votes and the bills signed after it ended keep being added for 45 days. Voting records start over for each Congress.
  • Times are converted from Eastern time. Senate nomination votes show the nominee but have no bill.
  • Vote types are grouped from the official question text: cloture (ending debate), nominations, passage (bills and resolutions, including suspension votes, concurring in the other chamber's changes and veto overrides), amendments, procedural motions, and other.
  • Key votes (the Votes tab's default, and the home page): final votes on passing a bill or resolution and on nominations. House "rules" (resolutions that only set the terms of debate for another bill) are left out even though they are resolutions; they, cloture, amendments and procedural motions are under All roll calls.
  • Headlines such as "House passed the … Act" are written from the official question, result and bill title by fixed rules; the official question and record are always shown with them. Only a final vote says a measure passed: a cloture vote reads "voted to end debate on …", an amendment vote "adopted an amendment to …", and so on. Bills are labeled the way Congress writes them (H.R. 2102, H.Res. 1247, S. 5).
  • Outcomes: "succeeded" means the official result says passed, agreed to, confirmed, adopted or sustained. A failed procedural motion can still be a win for the side that opposed it.

Party agreement

  • How divided a vote was (tags on vote cards), from Yea and Nay votes only. Close vote: the margin is 5% or less of all Yea and Nay votes. Split along party lines: most Democrats and most Republicans voted on opposite sides, with at least 90% of each party on its side. Broad agreement: most of both parties voted the same way, with at least two-thirds of each. Mixed support: none of these. Party tags need at least 10 Yea or Nay votes from each party.
  • On each vote, a party's majority position is whichever of Yea or Nay most of its members chose. Present and Not Voting are ignored, and votes where a party tied are left out.
  • Majorities opposed = most Democrats and most Republicans voted opposite ways; agreed = they voted the same way. Independents aren't counted as a party here.

Members' voting records

  • Counts cover every recorded vote in the member's own chamber this Congress on which the official record lists them, including as Not Voting.
  • Committees (member pages): current committee and subcommittee seats and titles (Chair, Ranking Member …) from the public-domain congress-legislators dataset, updated nightly.
  • Term and election (member pages): when the current term ends (and the Senate class) from the same dataset. The election date is worked out from it: terms end January 3, and federal elections are held the Tuesday after the first Monday in November of the year before. Once that election has been held we don't say who won; the winner takes office when the term ends. After a term ends, these lines stay hidden until the dataset lists the new term.
  • Voted on = Yea, Nay or Present. Voted with their party's majority compares their Yea or Nay with their party's majority position on that vote (as above); Independents aren't compared.
  • Missed votes = roll calls where the official record lists the member as Not Voting, out of the roll calls held while they served (Present is not a missed vote). Shown on member cards, in the member window and on Home.
  • Month by month (Voting Record): the same counts by the month of each vote (Eastern time): the share of roll calls missed, and how often their Yea or Nay matched their party's majority (months with fewer than 5 such votes are left out). A member is compared only with themselves.
  • Typical member = the median missed-vote share of the chamber's members (half missed less, half more), for context. Members with fewer than 20 roll calls (just sworn in) and delegates are left out of it. Delegates may vote only on some amendments, so we don't show them a missed-vote share.
  • The official record doesn't say why a vote was missed: illness, family, travel, campaigning and other duties look the same, so we don't suggest a reason.
  • We don't score, rank or rate members, and AI is never used to judge them. Missed votes are never sorted or listed as a ranking.

Issues pages

  • Month by month: recorded votes on the topic's measures by the month they were held (Eastern time), and laws by the month they were signed.
  • A topic's measures are bills with that CRS policy area that became law or got a recorded floor vote this Congress. Bills that never reached the floor aren't counted: most bills are never taken up, which isn't the same as being rejected.
  • Watchlists (Economy: tariffs & trade, taxes, budget & debt; Healthcare: coverage & costs, veterans' health, mental health & substance use; Foreign Affairs: Iran, Israel, foreign aid; Immigration: border security, legal status, visas & citizenship, asylum & refugees; Defense: troops & military families, veterans' benefits, defense budget & weapons, intelligence & surveillance) include a bill if CRS tagged it with a matching subject or its title names the topic, so they can include bills from other policy areas, such as defense, veterans' and spending bills. Each watchlist prints its exact rule.
  • A watchlist bill is focused if its title names the topic or CRS gave it 10 or fewer subjects; otherwise it's a broad bill (like an annual spending or defense bill) that includes the topic among many others.

Campaign finance

  • Totals as reported to the FEC by the member's own campaign committee for their next election period (the full 2-year House or 6-year Senate cycle), or the previous one if nothing is reported yet.
  • Not included: outside spending such as super PACs. A donor's state is only known for itemized gifts (over $200). We don't list employers or "top donors", because an individual's employer isn't the one giving.

What Congress did each week

  • Weeks run Monday to Sunday, Eastern time. The Overview shows the latest week with any roll-call vote or new law; each week has its own page and link.
  • Became law: bills whose "Became Public Law" action is dated that week. Passed / failed: roll-call votes on passage of a bill or resolution, by the official result. House rules (resolutions that only set the terms of debate for another bill) are listed apart under "Debate rules" and not counted as passed; their result reads "Agreed to". Bills that pass by voice vote or unanimous consent have no roll call, so they show up when they become law.
  • Closest votes: the three smallest Yea–Nay margins among passage, cloture, nomination and amendment votes; procedural motions are left out. Nominations: every Senate vote on a nomination, with the nominee as named in the vote record.
  • Key votes on the home page: the latest final votes on bills, resolutions and nominations in the latest week with votes. House "rules" (votes that set the terms of debate for another bill) and other procedural votes are left out; they're on the week page.
  • Your members come from the address you enter (looked up as described below) and are remembered only in your browser; their positions on key votes come from the official roll calls.
  • Nothing is picked by hand or by AI: every item follows these rules.

Your representatives

  • Your address is sent to the U.S. Census Bureau's geocoder to find your congressional district, using the district map of the Congress now in office. We don't store or log the address.
  • Phone numbers, websites and contact forms come from the public-domain congress-legislators dataset (each member's current term), updated nightly. Phone numbers are their Washington offices; not every member has a separate contact form, and their website links to one.
  • Your members on a bill (bill pages, once you've entered an address): whether they sponsored or cosponsored it (Congress.gov's cosponsor list, including withdrawn cosponsors; only members of the bill's own chamber can), and how they voted on its final roll calls: passage, accepting the other chamber's changes, veto overrides. Procedural steps (cloture, amendments, rules) aren't listed there; they're on the bill's Votes tab.

Following and "Since you last looked"

  • Follow a bill, a member or an Issues topic (up to 20 bills, 10 members, 10 topics). What you follow is saved only in this browser; nothing is sent to us, and clearing your browser data removes it.
  • Following saves what the item looked like then. Home compares that with today's data: a bill's new stage or latest action, a member's new roll-call votes and how many they missed (with their latest positions), a topic's new laws and measures. "Mark all as seen" saves today's state instead.

Coming up in the House

  • Home lists the House's weekly floor schedule, published by the Majority Leader's office at docs.house.gov, as it stood at our nightly update: bills that may be considered under suspension of the rules (fast track, two-thirds needed), under a rule, and other items. Items taken off the schedule are left out. Not every listed item gets a vote, and the House adds items during the week.
  • The Senate publishes no comparable list, so only House items appear; Senate votes show up once they happen. When no schedule is posted for the week (usually a recess), Home says so.
